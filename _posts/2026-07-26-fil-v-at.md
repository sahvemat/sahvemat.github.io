A very important part of chess technique is the ability to play, on a high
level, with bishop against knight or knight against bishop. There are two
parts to the problem: first, the ability to handle one of the two pieces on
a high level - to understand its strong possibilities in a given position;
second, knowing *when and how* to exchange a bishop for a knight (or vice
versa) - recognizing the right situations and making the correct decision.

These exchange decisions are very difficult, and players developed strong
personal preferences. In the Soviet Union there was an old school of
thought - the so-called **Chigorin school** - which believed the knight
was stronger than the bishop, tracing back to **Lasker, Emanuel - Chigorin,
Mikhail Ivanovich, Hastings, 06.08.1895**, in which Chigorin demonstrated
the advantage of two knights over two bishops. Players who preferred the
knight were nicknamed "Chigorinists," and some schools - the **Georgian
school**, for instance - became known for playing the knight at a very
high level.

It is not possible to simply follow Kasparov's joking rule that *"the
worst bishop is better than the worst knight"* - that isn't true at all.
Top players understood when to bring out the advantages of the bishop and
when to bring out the advantages of the knight, but it is far from easy.

Here is one of my own games, against (future) Grandmaster **Gennadi
Zaichik**, Soviet Team Championship, Riga - I played for Ukraine, he
played for Georgia.

After the game, Grandmaster **Eduard Gufeld** - a devoted
fan of the dark-squared bishop, famous for kissing it before trading it
away and thanking it "for its service" - joked with **Garry Kasparov**,
who played the Gruenfeld/King's Indian and had also exchanged his own
g7-bishop in a game: *"Garry, what you did is a crime!"* Gufeld then came
to our board and said: *"Oh, young guys, you don't know how to play with
bishops - how is it possible to spoil a position like this?"*

**Tigran Petrosian**, standing nearby, laughed and said: *"Don't listen to
Eduard, young guys - after 23...c5 this position is theoretically lost.
It's lost because you have no counterplay and the bishop is bad."* He
asked if I understood why; I said no. Petrosian explained: I had broken
**Capablanca's rule** - *if you have the bishop, put your pawns on the
color opposite to your bishop's squares.* It is a rule, and if you break
it, you get punished. He added: if queens were still on the board (say, a
Black queen on b6 and a White queen on c4), then 23...c5 would actually
have been logical, opening an attack on White's king - and in that case
the bishop would have been much stronger than the knight.

A very instructive story: **23...c5 lost the game because it broke
Capablanca's rule.** Follow it, and you succeed. Break it, and you lose
the game - as I did.

{ Another difficult decision: *to exchange, or not to exchange.* A few years
later, when I was already a serious grandmaster, I played this game against
Grandmaster **Alexander Chernin** at the **Capablanca Memorial** in
Cienfuegos. It was a **French Defense**, and we had somehow repeated the
outlines of a famous **Petrosian - Spassky** game from their World

advantage here, and I started to calculate variations.

I later gave this exact position as a test to a number of my pupils -
several of them grandmasters - to find the first move. }
16. Bxf6 $1 $16 { The first move I calculated was the natural developing

does not actually favor White - see the note.

I then tried 16.Ne5, aiming to stop *...Nd7*. But that runs into a
tactical problem - see the note.

So calculation after calculation showed that these logical-looking moves
give nothing. I thought for half an hour, unable to understand why. Then I
understood: Black's plan is *...Nd7*, and the **knight is Black's key
defensive piece** - it protects c5 and can reroute to d6. *That knight has
to be destroyed first.* Hence: **16.Bxf6!**, eliminating the defender
before anything else. }
(16. Rd1 {[%cal Rd1d3,Rd3a3]} Nd7 17. Bxe7 Kxe7 18. Rd3 Rhc8 19. Ra3 Nb6

protected the a7-weakness. Nothing comes of White's attack. }) (16. Ne5
Nd7 17. Bxe7 Nxe5 18. Rxc5 Nd7 { [%csl Rc5,Re7] A **double attack** - the
rook on c5 is hit and White is losing the exchange. })
16... gxf6 17. Rd1 { A normal, healthy move now - only good *because* the
defending knight is already gone and Black's kingside structure is
ruined. } Rb8 (17... Rd8 18. Rxd8+ Kxd8 19. Ra4 $18 { and the a7-pawn is
lost. }) 18. Rd3 $1 {[%cal Rd3a3,Ra3a7]} { Same idea as before: come to
a3 and attack the weakness. } Rb4 {[%csl Rd4,Re5,Rf3,Rg5,Rh4]} { A tricky
idea - Black wants to stop *Ra3* by improving his pawn structure. If the
pawns get to coordinate and protect each other (even a5 supporting b4),
Ra3 is no longer possible. } 19. Nd2 { Time to bring another piece into
the game - the knight was not exactly passive, but at this moment it was
**helpless**, so it has to be included.

Here I applied the so-called **Makogonov rule** - named for the famous
Soviet trainer (one of Garry Kasparov's first serious coaches): *when
there is no direct tactic available, find your worst-placed piece and
improve it.* What was my worst piece? Without any deep calculation, I
simply improved it - the king heads to f3. } (19. Rxb4 cxb4
{[%cal Ra7a5]}) 19... Rg8 20. g3 Rxc4 21. Nxc4 Rg4 22. f4 h5 (22... e5
23. h3 {[%cal Rg4c4]} Rg6 24. fxe5 $18) 23. Kd2 $1 $18 (23. Ra3 h4
24. Rxa7 hxg3 25. hxg3 Rxg3 26. a4 { White is clearly better here too, but
in such situations it is better to use safer methods that give the
opponent no counterplay. }) 23... h4 24. Ke2 hxg3 25. hxg3 Rg6 26. Ra3
Rh6 27. Kf3 Rh1 (27... Rh2 28. c3 {[%csl Re7]}) 28. Rxa7 { Black resigned
here - there is no defense against *Rxa7* combined with the advance of
the a-pawn. } 1-0
</pgn>

**And look at the role of the bishop**: it never made a single move in
this entire game.

I gave this position to many of my pupils, including several strong
grandmasters, in two stages: first, an immediate answer, testing pure
intuition and feel; then, after five minutes of thinking, a chance to
confirm or change that first move. Only **about 20%** of them found
*16.Bxf6!*

It is a very difficult decision to find. My own path to it was this: once
I understood that Black's best piece was the knight, and Black's main
threat was *...Nd7* (after which Black defends everything), the way to
find the move was to **eliminate the strongest piece** - the one
threatening to begin the unpleasant defensive maneuver. 

Everybody would like to know: *when is the knight really better than the
bishop, and when is the bishop better than the knight?* In open positions,
the bishop's role is naturally significant. The knight is strong when it
controls squares in a **closed** position that are simply not available to
the opponent's bishop.

## The Doubled-Pawn Rule

That much is clear, but there is an important feature rarely explained in
books, and I will show you how it works in this game. White here was
**Boleslavsky** - at the time one of the world's top five or ten players; a
few years later he played a Candidates final match against **Bronstein**,
which is to say he was, for a stretch, essentially the world's
**number three**.

So: in the starting position of this example, one could say Black was
already better, but the *reason* wasn't obvious. Now it can be explained:
**when there are doubled pawns in the position, the knight is generally
much stronger than the bishop.** This is exactly why Nimzowitsch's
revolutionary idea in his own defense was not to wait passively - he
would voluntarily take on c3, inflict doubled pawns on the opponent, and
then successfully attack them. It's a real rule, and for some reason it
is rarely explained in the books: when there are doubled pawns on the
board, the knight's advantage over the bishop becomes huge.

## Doubled Pawns in Closed Positions

Once again: doubled pawns are a huge reason to be extremely careful,
because in such situations the power of the knight becomes tremendous -
and it doesn't matter whether the position is open or closed. In a
closed position, it's actually even worse.

better when there are doubled pawns in the opponent's position. In such

technically make use of the knight's advantage over the bishop.

players over the years, not only Fischer here - Garry Kasparov exploited
essentially the same idea in one of his own games, reaching a similar
position where Black could win the d4-pawn with *...Nf5*, though that
particular game actually ended in a draw. A very similar structure also
came up in a Karpov-Kasparov game, which Karpov won brilliantly. But here
Fischer plays even better. } 50. Kd3 (50. Bxg3 Kxg3 51. Kf1 f3 52. gxf3
Kxf3 {[%cal Gf3e3]}) 50... Nf5 (50... f3 51. gxf3+ Kxf3 {[%cal Gg3f5]}) 51.
Bf2 Nh4 { And now the pawn ending would once again be lost in the very
same Rubinstein manner - king to g3, king to f1, ...f3, exchanges, and
Black's king marches in and wins the d4-pawn. } 52. a5 (52. Bxh4 Kxh4 53.
Ke2 Kg3 54. Kf1 f3 55. gxf3 Kxf3 {[%cal Gf3e3]}) 52... Nxg2 { Now the task
becomes very easy. } 53. Kc3 Kf3 54. Bg1 Ke2 55. Bh2 f3 56. Bg3 Ne3 {
[%cal Ge3f5]} { The point being *...Nf5* next, kicking away the bishop and
promoting the pawn. } 0-1
</pgn>

Once again, what is very typical and very important for the technique of
knight versus bishop: first, a **symmetrical pawn structure**; second,
pawns placed - per Capablanca's rule - on the squares of the opponent's
bishop; and third, technically repositioning the knight again and again,
to attack the opponent's pawns from different angles. This last part is
the most difficult part of the whole business.

knight-vs-bishop position with doubled pawns. Even a player as great as
Tal could have trouble understanding this kind of position.

## The Long Technique

Now I want to show you a really incredible technique in
the endgame: how it's possible to exploit, on one flank, the advantage of
the knight over the bishop when the pawns are doubled. This is from the
**Soviet Zonal tournament, 1978**.

## Closing Thoughts

So remember: whenever you're trying to exploit the advantage of the
knight over the bishop, or preparing it through some exchange, keep this
in mind - in a symmetrical position, the power of the knight increases;
but the power of the knight increases far more when you manage to create
doubled pawns in the opponent's position. In such cases the knight
becomes terribly strong, and for some strange reason this isn't written
about in most of the books - even though top players have known this,
and known how to exploit it, since as far back as the 1920s.
